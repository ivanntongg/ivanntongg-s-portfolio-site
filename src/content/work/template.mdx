{/*
  HOW TO USE THIS TEMPLATE

  The headings below are the spine of a case study that survives a portfolio
  review. Keep them in this order. The order is the argument: a reviewer should
  be able to read only the headings and know whether you think well.

  Length target: 700 to 1,200 words. Longer than that and it stops being read.
  Cut process documentation before you cut reasoning.

  The three questions every reviewer is actually asking:
    1. Did you understand the real problem, or the one you were handed?
    2. What did you decide, and what did you give up to decide it?
    3. How do you know it worked?

  Most portfolios answer none of these. They show artefacts instead.
*/}

## The situation

Two or three sentences. What the product was, who used it, and what state it
was in when you arrived. Concrete and unglamorous. No mission statements.

## The real problem

This is the section that separates strong portfolios from competent ones.
State the brief you were given, then state what you found was actually wrong,
and show the evidence that moved you from one to the other. If the brief was
correct, say so and say how you confirmed it.

Reviewers hire the ability to reframe. Show it once, clearly, here.

## Constraints

What you could not change: the deadline, the legacy data model, the two
engineers, the regulation, the exec who had already promised it. Design
decisions are only legible against their constraints. A case study without
constraints reads as fiction.

## What I decided

The core of the piece. Two or three decisions, not ten. For each one:

- the options you weighed
- what you picked
- **what you gave up**, stated plainly

That last item is the one everyone omits, and it is the one that proves you
were making decisions rather than producing screens.

## What I got wrong

One real thing. Something you shipped and had to walk back, an assumption that
did not hold, a test that embarrassed you. This section costs nothing and buys
more credibility than the rest of the page combined. Interviewers ask about it
every time, which means you get to control the question.

## Outcome

Numbers where you have them, honest hedging where you do not. "Shipped to 1.4k
teams; onboarding tickets fell 40% over the following quarter, though we also
changed pricing in the same window" is far stronger than an unqualified claim,
because it shows you know what a confound is.

If you have no metrics, say what you would have measured and why you could not.

## What I would do next

Two sentences. Shows you are still thinking about it, and gives the interviewer
somewhere obvious to take the conversation.
